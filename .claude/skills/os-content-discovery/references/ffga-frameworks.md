# FFGA Frameworks — psychology sources for emotional-driver work

FFGAs (Fears, Frustrations, Goals, Aspirations) are first-person emotional statements that capture how an audience actually feels about a specific subtopic. The `drivers` mode produces 8 per run (2 of each type) by drawing on a small set of psychology and persuasion frameworks for depth.

This reference documents those frameworks and how they inform good FFGA generation.

## The four FFGA types

**Fears** — negative feelings about what *might* happen, what they might lose, what they're afraid of being seen as. Forward-looking and conditional. *"I'm terrified that if I niche down too hard I'll cap out my growth."*

**Frustrations** — negative feelings about what's *already happening*, what isn't working, what they're stuck on right now. Present-tense and concrete. *"My engagement is all over the place and I have no idea what's actually working."*

**Goals** — positive desired outcomes, often tactical or operational. What they want to achieve. *"I want a clear answer to what I post about so I stop second-guessing every piece."*

**Aspirations** — positive desired states of being, often identity-level or transformational. Who they want to become. *"I want to be the person someone immediately thinks of when their friend says 'do you know anyone who does X?'"*

A common drift mode is producing aspirations that are just inverse-frustrations. *"I want my content to get engagement"* as the mirror of *"I'm frustrated by low engagement"* is too thin. Each FFGA should offer a distinct emotional angle, not a sign-flip of an earlier one.

## Source frameworks

The following frameworks help generate FFGAs with depth and variety beyond surface-level feelings.

**Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions.** Robert Plutchik's eight primary emotions (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation) and their dyads. Useful for finding the precise emotional name for a feeling — *anticipation* and *trust* often hide behind generic *"want"* statements, and *disgust* often hides behind generic *"frustration."* The wheel gives FFGAs more emotional specificity than the four-bucket structure alone.

**Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs.** Physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization. Useful for level-of-need calibration. Surface-level FFGAs often live at the esteem level (*"I want to be seen as competent"*). Deeper FFGAs reach for safety (*"I'm scared this is the wrong path and I'll waste years"*) or self-actualization (*"I want to feel like I'm doing what I was meant to do"*). The mix of levels makes FFGAs feel multi-dimensional.

**5 Whys.** Toyota's root-cause technique applied emotionally. The first feeling you name is rarely the actual driver — keep asking why until you reach the emotion that's actually driving it. Surface: *"I'm frustrated my content isn't growing."* Why? *"Because I'm working hard and not seeing results."* Why does that bother you? *"Because I'm starting to wonder if I picked the wrong path."* The third or fourth why often produces the FFGA that resonates.

**Cialdini's Persuasion Principles.** Reciprocity, commitment / consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Useful for spotting where the audience's feelings align with persuasion levers — *"Everyone in my network is ahead of me"* (social proof), *"I committed to this and now I have to make it work"* (commitment / consistency), *"Time is running out"* (scarcity). FFGAs that touch persuasion principles tend to convert better in downstream drafting.

**Fogg Behavior Model.** B = MAT (Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Trigger). Useful for spotting friction-related FFGAs. The audience often has motivation but lacks ability or trigger. *"I want to post consistently but I never know what to write about"* is a classic ability-gap frustration.

**Empathy Maps.** Says / Thinks / Feels / Does. Useful for distinguishing what the audience *says* (in surveys, on calls) from what they *think* (privately). FFGAs target the *thinks* layer — what they would say to a therapist or close friend, not what they would say in a polite product survey.

## How the frameworks combine

The frameworks aren't templates — the mode doesn't run through each one mechanically. They're a mental library the mode samples from when generating each FFGA.

A useful pattern: for each FFGA slot, briefly check whether the candidate statement names a specific emotion (Plutchik), reaches a specific need level (Maslow), captures a real driver rather than the surface feeling (5 Whys), aligns with a persuasion lever (Cialdini), reflects a real friction point (Fogg), and lives in the *thinks / feels* register rather than *says* (Empathy Map). A statement that hits 3-4 of those tends to be a strong FFGA.

A statement that hits 0-1 tends to be generic. *"I want to grow my business"* fails most checks — vague emotion, surface-level, not in audience voice. *"I'm exhausted from constantly trying to meet others' expectations and I want to rediscover what makes me happy"* hits emotional specificity (exhaustion + longing), need level (self-actualization), depth (the why beneath the surface), and audience voice.

## First-person voice

The single most important craft constraint. FFGAs are written in the audience's first-person voice — the way someone would describe the feeling to a therapist or close friend.

*"They feel exhausted from trying to meet others' expectations"* — third-person, dry, doesn't survive into drafted prose.

*"I'm exhausted from constantly trying to meet others' expectations"* — first-person, has a pulse, drops directly into drafted prose as a pain-triplet line or a hook setup.

The FFGA's job is to be drafting-ready. Third-person FFGAs fail that test.
