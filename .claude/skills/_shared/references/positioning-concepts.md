# Positioning Concepts — Reference

Shared domain reference for skills that surface, structure, or articulate a business's positioning. Loaded by multiple skills (`biz-strategy`, `offer`, future `content-strategy`) for the same purpose: producing consistent positioning language across channels (elevator pitch, sales page, one-pager, ad copy, offer vehicle).

## USP — Unique Selling Proposition

Classical direct-response concept (Rosser Reeves, *Reality in Advertising*). A USP answers: *why should a prospect choose you over every other option, including doing nothing?*

A complete USP has four components:

1. **Unique belief or philosophy** — what the practitioner believes about the domain that most competitors don't
2. **Distinct approach or process** — what they do differently in the work itself
3. **Unmatched promise or experience** — what specific outcome they stand behind
4. **Unlike Statement** — the contrastive claim: *"Unlike [common alternative], we [unique differentiator]"*

A USP missing any component feels incomplete. Missing the Unlike Statement especially — generic positioning is often what you get when the Unlike Statement is implicit or absent.

## NTPV — Niche, Transformation, Promise, Vehicle

A structural messaging model used to produce consistent positioning across channels. Each element is specific and named, not abstract.

| Element | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Niche** | Who specifically do you serve? | "Solopreneur coaches scaling past $10K MRR" — not "coaches" |
| **Transformation** | What do they become? | "Coaches who run leveraged businesses" — not "successful coaches" |
| **Promise** | What outcome do you stand behind? | "Reliable $30K+ months without launch-induced chaos" — not "grow your business" |
| **Vehicle** | How do you deliver the transformation? | "The Leveraged Offer Engine — a three-phase productization system" — not "coaching" |

NTPV shows up in sales pages, one-pagers, elevator pitches, ad copy, and offer vehicles. Each channel may foreground a different element, but all four should be consistent and recoverable.

## The "Unlike Statement" pattern

Structural pattern: *"Unlike [common alternative], we [unique differentiator]."*

The strength of an Unlike Statement depends on:

- **Named alternative.** "Unlike most coaches" is generic; "Unlike content-heavy courses that take 12 weeks and leave you stalled at module 3" is specific. Name the specific shape of the alternative.
- **Single differentiator.** One clear point of contrast, not a list. Lists dilute the claim.
- **Buyer-recognizable.** The prospect should see the alternative in their own prior experience. If the Unlike Statement describes an alternative they've never considered, it lands flat.

## Elevator Pitch structure

Canonical one-sentence positioning:

> *"I help [who] with [what] so they can [why/value]. [Optionally: via [unique mechanism or vehicle]]. [Optionally: without [unwanted situation]]."*

Rules:

- Must feel natural to say aloud (no jargon, no corporate phrasing).
- Specific [who] — same tight-niche discipline as NTPV.
- Concrete [why/value] — what actually changes for them.
- The vehicle addition works when the mechanism has a nameable shape; skip when forcing it produces awkward phrasing.
- The "without" clause is optional but often strong — it surfaces a feared cost or alternative-failure the prospect is actively avoiding.

## Value Articulation Story — 7-beat format

Narrative structure for longer-form positioning (sales pages, VSL openings, pitch decks, offer messaging). Each beat is a single sentence; together they form a belief-shifting arc.

1. **Most** [target audience] [current struggle or approach]
2. **And that's a problem because** [why the current approach fails]
3. **Because of that,** [negative consequences]
4. **Which means** [emotional / practical impact]
5. **On the other hand,** [your different approach]
6. **So that** [immediate benefits]
7. **And consequently,** [ultimate transformation]

The format is related to StoryBrand (before/after) and older direct-response templates. The discipline is completing all seven beats — skipping beats or collapsing them produces uneven narrative pressure.

This format is also used as the **Problem Promise Paragraph (PPP)** in offer contexts — same 7-beat structure, applied specifically to offer messaging for VSLs, landing pages, and launch emails.

## Zone of Genius vs. Zone of Competence

- **Zone of Genius** — what the practitioner does uniquely well, loves to do, and creates disproportionate results from. Should anchor the positioning. (Gay Hendricks, *The Big Leap*.)
- **Zone of Competence** — what they can do, may be known for, but doesn't produce outsized results. Should be downplayed or removed from positioning.

Practitioners often default to positioning themselves around the Zone of Competence because it's broader and safer. Positioning skills surface the Zone of Genius and center the positioning on it.

Tell: when the user talks about one area of their work, energy rises noticeably (they get animated, language gets specific, stories surface unprompted). That's Zone of Genius. When they talk about another area with competent-but-flat description, that's Zone of Competence.

## Non-Genius Work

The flip side of Zone of Genius — what the practitioner refuses to do, even if capable. Explicit non-genius work is a positioning asset: it signals discipline and makes the positive offer more credible by defining its edges.

Examples:
- "I don't coach anyone under $10K MRR — they need a different mechanism first"
- "I don't do done-for-you copywriting — the clients who need that aren't my clients"
- "I don't teach tactics without strategy — tactics alone produce exhaustion"

The "I don't" clauses often land stronger than the "I do" clauses because they demonstrate judgment.

## Opening question strategy

Positioning interviews open with an *invitation to reflect*, not a diagnostic question. Classic contrast:

- Closed/diagnostic: *"What's your business model?"* → produces one-line answer
- Invitation: *"In a general sense, what kind of business are you in?"* → produces a paragraph that reveals how they think about themselves

## Silent observation — what to track without revealing

During positioning interviews, track internally without narrating to the user:

- **Language patterns** — recurring phrases, metaphors, emotional registers
- **Zone of Genius spikes** — where energy rises
- **Fuzzy vs. clear articulation** — which parts of their positioning they've already worked out vs. still figuring out
- **Tangible vs. intangible transformations** — specific outcomes vs. feeling-level outcomes
- **Belief statements that could serve as positioning** — claims they make in passing
- **Precise audience markers and use-case signals**
- **Tension points and contrast** — where they disagree with their industry
- **USP indicators** — uncommon offers, traits, beliefs
- **NTPV structure** — each of the four elements as they surface
- **Success patterns and proof points**
- **Common objections and misconceptions they face**

Revealing the tracking mid-interview causes the user to perform for the tracking rather than speaking honestly.

## Mirror-their-language discipline

The final positioning blueprint uses the user's exact phrasing wherever possible. Analyst-polished language, even when technically more precise, loses the voice the user will actually speak in. Generic buzzwords ("empower," "transform," "align") should be stripped unless the user demonstrably uses them with specific intent.

Tradeoff: grammatical correctness sometimes bends to preserve voice. That's the right tradeoff.

## Malleability note

Reference-grade but malleable. The concepts (USP, NTPV, Unlike Statement, Elevator Pitch, Value Articulation Story, Zone of Genius) are direct-response / StoryBrand / Hendricks canon and should not be renamed or restructured. What IS malleable:

- **Which output format(s) a skill produces.** `positioning` mode produces all six; `os-offer/modes/offer-vehicle` might use only NTPV + Unlike Statement + Elevator Pitch. Skills pick the subset that fits the job.
- **Beat exactness for Value Articulation Story.** Seven beats is the discipline, but if the input genuinely collapses two beats (e.g., *"And that's a problem because... which means..."* flow together in a tight niche), the compression is acceptable. Don't fabricate beats to hit seven.
- **Unlike Statement alternative identification.** The specific "common alternative" varies dramatically by niche. The pattern holds; the content is always skill-specific.

Skills that diverge should note the reason in their Design Rationale.
