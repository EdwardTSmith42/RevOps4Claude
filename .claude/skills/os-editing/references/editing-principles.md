# Editing Principles

Cross-mode principles for `editing`. Every mode loads or honors these. Stated here once so they don't get repeated in every mode file.

## Mirror the author's language

Don't introduce vocabulary the source doesn't use. Suggested rewrites should feel like the author wrote them. If the writer never says "leverage," don't put "leverage" in a suggested edit. If they never use semicolons, don't add them.

This is a discipline against editors imposing taste. The author has a voice. The edit's job is to surface what the writer was trying to do, not redirect it.

## Specific over generic

Vagueness is the most common writing problem and the hardest to self-diagnose. Prefer concrete nouns, named examples, exact numbers, specific outcomes.

- "Many businesses see results" → "Most B2B SaaS companies see a 14-22% lift"
- "Get more opens" → "2x open rates"
- "Industry professional" → "10+ years of selling, sourced or closed $10M+ in revenue"

The four questions every piece of content should answer (from Specific Writing Helper):
1. Why read it?
2. What will they learn?
3. How will it help?
4. Why should they trust you?

If the draft can't answer any of these specifically, that's where the edit goes.

## Preserve voice

A clean edit that homogenizes voice is worse than a rough draft that's distinctively the writer's. Voice survives is the main constraint.

- If the writer uses fragments, fragments are part of the voice.
- If the writer's prose runs in triplets, triplets are part of the voice.
- If the writer hedges as a stylistic choice, hedge-cutting changes voice.
- If the writer breaks rules deliberately for tone or character (especially in fiction), the rule-break is the voice.

Voice-sensitive editing means catching when an "improvement" would erase what makes the writing the writer's.

## Suggest, don't dictate

The author has final say. Frame edits as options where voice or style is at issue. For mechanical issues (subject-verb agreement, typos), one correction is fine. For style or voice issues, offer 1-2 alternatives so the author picks the fit.

This applies especially in fiction, where voice judgments are often subjective. Two options respect agency. One option dictates.

## Don't edit for the sake of editing

If a sentence is fine, leave it alone. The urge to "improve" everything degrades clean writing.

A suggestion list that flags every paragraph is a tell that the editor is performing thoroughness rather than diagnosing actual issues. Restraint is craft.

## Read the whole piece before editing any of it

Local edits without global understanding produce inconsistent voice and broken argument flow. The first read is for argument, voice, and shape. The second read is for line-level work. Editors who skip the first read produce edits that work in isolation but fail across the draft.

## Don't return passages unchanged

Suggestion-list output focuses on actionable items. A wall of "looks fine" entries hides the real issues. If a passage is clean, omit it from the list. The list is the report. If the list is short, the writing is in better shape than the editor expected.

This rule is enforced in the suggestion-list template's output discipline and applies to `suggest-edits`, parts of `assessment` (recommendations), and `fiction-edit`.

## Stay grounded in mechanics, not just messaging

A messaging tweak without mechanical change produces temporary lift, not durable improvement. When `assessment` flags low scores, prioritize structural and mechanical issues over surface-level rewordings — the structural fix carries.

## Don't recommend the easy thing without justification

The lazy recommendation — "add a hook," "use stronger verbs," "be more specific" — is generic enough to be useless. Every recommendation must be specific (cite the passage), contextualized (why does this matter for THIS draft), and prescriptive (what specifically to do).

This applies most directly to `assessment`'s prioritized recommendations and to `suggest-edits` lens output.

## The author is unoffendable

Most authors who invoke editing modes are looking for blunt, useful diagnosis — not reassurance. Be direct. Don't pad recommendations with cushioning ("This is great, but maybe consider..."). Skip the praise unless it's specifically warranted and useful (e.g., "the structural choice in paragraph 3 is doing real work — keep it").

This is calibrated to the user, not the prose. If the user is a professional working on commercial content, blunt is right. If the user is a beginner who has flagged anxiety, calibrate gentler — but still useful.
