# Fiction Editing Layers

The 4-layer process for `modes/fiction-edit`. Layered because fiction editing has distinct concerns at distinct granularities, and running them in this order — with the Voice-Sensitive Filter as the third layer specifically — produces edits that don't homogenize voice.

## Why layered, not unified

A unified-pass fiction editor (one read, all concerns at once) tends to over-correct: line-level "improvements" erase the voice quirks that make the prose the writer's. The 4-layer process separates concerns, runs them in order of cost-of-fix (cheap to expensive), and inserts a voice-protection step before editorial-judgment work begins.

## The four layers

### Layer 1 — Proofreader (Surface Clean-Up)

The cheapest fixes. Pure mechanical correctness.

- Fix spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors
- Remove double spaces, inconsistent dashes, or extra line breaks
- Apply standard rules unless clearly overridden by style

Layer 1 outputs are usually accepted without negotiation. If the writer uses an em-dash style consistently, that's style, not error — leave it. If they use double-spaces after periods consistently, that's choice (some writers do), leave it. The layer catches *errors*, not *deviations from convention*.

### Layer 2 — Line Editor (Flow + Clarity)

Sentence-level prose work.

- Break up overly long or tangled sentences
- Improve rhythm and readability
- Replace vague or repetitive phrasing
- Tighten redundancies and filler words

Layer 2 is where most prose improvement happens. It's also where most voice-erosion happens — line edits that "improve" prose can replace the writer's idiosyncratic rhythm with editor-default rhythm. Hence the next layer.

### Layer 3 — Voice-Sensitive Filter (Style Awareness)

Reviews Layer 2's suggestions and pushes back where they would erase voice.

- Don't over-polish sentences that intentionally break rules for tone or character
- Respect stylistic quirks unless they interfere with clarity or pacing
- Make edits that enhance voice, not erase it

**The architectural insight:** This layer exists because Layer 2 (Line Editor), run alone, will over-correct. Voice is fragile — sentence rhythm, fragment use, repetition for effect, deliberate rule-breaks — and Layer 2 doesn't know what's voice and what's drift. Layer 3 is the editor's check against their own line-editing instinct.

Layer 3 is what holds the whole process together. Without it, fiction editing produces voice-homogenized prose that reads cleaner but worse.

Common Layer 3 push-backs:
- A fragment Layer 2 wanted to complete is a voice signature — leave it
- A repeated word Layer 2 flagged is doing rhythmic work — leave it
- A run-on Layer 2 wanted to split is the character's voice in close-third POV — leave it
- A "vague" verb Layer 2 wanted to sharpen is ambient atmospheric, not weak — leave it

When Layer 3 fires, the suggested edit gets dropped, downgraded to optional ("consider this alternative if you want a different rhythm here, but the original is doing work"), or flagged as a subjective choice.

### Layer 4 — Editorial Judgment

The most expensive fixes. Manuscript-level concerns.

- If something is technically correct but still awkward, fix it
- Use comments sparingly to flag author choices or alternatives
- Make suggestions to improve general flow and prose so it reads like a fluent native speaker
- Edit like a pro: confident, honest, efficient

Layer 4 also surfaces developmental observations — narrative flow, character consistency, scene purpose, motivation clarity, pacing. These are NOT rewrites. They're diagnostic notes the author can act on.

## Developmental observation patterns

Common Layer 4 developmental flags (from source examples):

- **Narrative Flow:** *"Chapter opens with a flashback, but reader has no grounding in present-day context."* Suggestion: insert short present-day anchor before flashback.
- **Character Consistency:** *"In chapter 3, the protagonist forgives the antagonist quickly, despite major betrayal."* Suggestion: expand emotional processing or introduce hesitation.
- **Scene Purpose / Redundancy:** *"Two back-to-back scenes both show the protagonist brooding alone."* Suggestion: combine or reframe one to move plot.
- **Motivation Clarity Gap:** *"Secondary character risks their life without a clear reason."* Suggestion: insert internal thought or earlier dialogue hinting at loyalty / unresolved guilt.
- **Pacing:** *"Opening chapter includes extensive worldbuilding before any action."* Suggestion: weave exposition into unfolding scene or start mid-conflict.

Developmental notes don't include rewrites — they propose a direction for the author to consider. The author owns the scene. The editor owns the diagnostic.

## Genre voice signals (calibrate Layer 3 by genre)

The Voice-Sensitive Filter is genre-calibrated — what registers as voice signal in noir reads as drift in literary minimalism, and vice versa. Identify the manuscript's genre during Layer 1's reading pass and adjust Layer 3's protection thresholds accordingly. The signals below are protection candidates. Flagging them as Layer 2 issues is usually wrong unless the manuscript itself contradicts the genre.

**Noir / dark urban fantasy / hard-boiled crime:**
- Paratactic short-short-short rhythms (sentence beats matching tough-guy cadence)
- Deferred subject in opening sentences (atmosphere first, character second)
- Sensory grime and texture words ("fetid," "grime-covered," "burnt orange") used as setting, not vagueness
- Slang and code-switch (street slang, supernatural-world vocabulary, period-appropriate profanity) — protect, don't sanitize
- Italicized interior thought, especially mid-action (*"See a knife, run."*)
- Fragments as punch ("Not just rhetoric, either.")
- Comma-spliced run-ons in interior voice (the run-on IS the cadence)

**Literary / quiet realism / minimalism:**
- Long lyrical sentences with multiple subordinate clauses
- Free indirect discourse (narrative voice merging with character interiority)
- Ambiguity tolerance (intentional withholding, unresolved imagery)
- Repeated words used as motif (not as Layer 2 redundancy)
- White-space pacing and one-sentence paragraphs as deliberate rhythm
- Plain-stated emotion held just under the surface

**Genre fantasy / sci-fi / urban fantasy:**
- Invented vocabulary tolerance (made-up nouns, magical-system jargon, technobabble)
- Worldbuilding asides as scene texture (one or two sentences mid-narrative explaining a society / system / creature)
- Power-system specificity (the rules of the magic / tech are voice — protect specifics)
- Genre clichés as setting, not problem (a sword fight, a spell duel, a shootout aren't plot clichés in their genre)

**YA / Middle Grade:**
- Voice-forward first-person tics ("Like, literally," "anyway," "or whatever")
- Higher dialogue ratio and shorter narration spans
- Direct-address asides to the reader
- Contemporary slang the genre demands

**Romance:**
- Sensory specificity in physical description (touch, scent, sound) is genre register
- Tension-pacing sentences that linger on a single moment
- Internal monologue threaded through dialogue scenes

The list isn't exhaustive. The principle: identify the genre, then ask "if this signal were absent, would readers of THIS genre miss it?" If yes, protect.

When the manuscript signals one genre but the prose imitates without earning (e.g., a debut writer overusing noir rhythm without the underlying cadence), Layer 3's job becomes harder — distinguishing *real voice* from *imitative voice*. The test: does the rhythm pay off across the chapter, or does it appear in isolated sentences that read like attempts? Imitative voice surfaces in sentences that try too hard while the rest of the prose drifts toward editor-default. Real voice is consistent. Flag imitative passages as Layer 2 candidates with rationale: "this reads as an attempt at [genre] rhythm without the consistency to land it — consider whether the intent is voice or drift."

## Style of edits across all layers

- **Clean and confident.** Don't hedge.
- **Occasionally witty or sharp** when warranted, but never snide.
- **Minimal explanation** unless requested.
- **Reads like a manuscript touched by a pro, not an algorithm.**

## What the editor does NOT do

**Do not rewrite or reimagine the author's scenes.** The job is to clean, polish, and elevate the original — not to ghostwrite. Keep the author's setup, characters, beats, and outcomes intact.

If the manuscript needs ghostwriting (whole scenes rewritten), that's outside the scope of the editing skill. Tell the author.

## Output

See `../templates/fiction-sample-edit.md` for the format. Most fiction-edit output uses the Sample Edit Format (Original / Rationale / Suggested Edits) because side-by-side comparison is what makes voice and style judgments verifiable.

For sections that don't need explanation, in-line edited manuscript (showing the cleaned version without markup) is the preferred default — it gives the author a clean draft to work from.

For developmental observations, use the looser format (Issue type / Observation / Suggestion) without rewrites.
