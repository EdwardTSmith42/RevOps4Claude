# Tone Dimensions

The 4-dimensional tone framework for `modes/tone-profile`. Each dimension is a continuous axis with neutral midpoint and labeled extremes.

## The four dimensions

### 1. Formal / Casual

Is the writing formal? Informal? Casual?

**Note:** Casual and conversational are not necessarily synonymous, but they often appear together. A piece can be casual-not-conversational (chat slang, informal vocabulary, but distant address) or conversational-not-casual (direct address, dialogic, but formal register).

**Markers of formal:** Latinate vocabulary, complex syntax, distant address (third person, "one"), technical or industry-specific terms, complete sentences with subordinate clauses, formal connectors ("however," "moreover," "thus").

**Markers of casual:** Anglo-Saxon vocabulary, shorter sentences, contractions ("we're," "don't," "it's"), direct address ("you"), idiomatic phrasing, fragments, em dashes for asides.

### 2. Serious / Funny

Is the writer trying to be humorous? Or is the subject approached in a serious way?

**Note:** This dimension scores the *attempt* at humor, not whether the jokes land. A piece can score high on "funny" if it attempts humor, even if a particular joke falls flat.

**Markers of serious:** Plain statement of fact, measured pacing, consequential framing, no wordplay, no asides, no self-deprecation as comic relief.

**Markers of funny:** Wordplay, irony, hyperbole, deliberate absurdity, self-deprecation as comedic device, asides that puncture the seriousness, comic timing in pacing.

### 3. Respectful / Irreverent

Does the writer approach the subject in a respectful way, or take an irreverent approach?

**Note:** In practice, most irreverent tones are irreverent about the *subject matter*, in an effort to set the writer apart from competitors or convention. They are not usually intentionally irreverent or offensive to the *reader*.

**Markers of respectful:** Treats authorities as authoritative, doesn't sneer at conventions, addresses the reader as a peer, avoids mockery.

**Markers of irreverent:** Pokes at industry conventions, names the emperor's lack of clothes, treats authorities skeptically, uses mockery or satire about the subject matter.

### 4. Matter-of-Fact / Enthusiastic

Does the writer seem to be enthusiastic about the subject? Is the writing dry and matter-of-fact?

**Note:** "Enthusiasm" here means emotion more than excitement. A piece about a difficult topic can still register as enthusiastic if the writer is emotionally engaged, even if the emotion is grief or anger rather than excitement.

**Markers of matter-of-fact:** Plain statement, even pacing, neutral diction, neutral punctuation (period-heavy), no rhetorical intensifiers.

**Markers of enthusiastic:** Emotional verbs and adjectives, rhetorical questions, exclamation marks (or close stylistic equivalents), italics for emphasis, varied pacing with bursts.

## Scoring

Each dimension scored 0-10, with **5 = neutral**, 0 = extremely toward the left pole, 10 = extremely toward the right pole.

Score bands and labels:
- **0-1**: extremely [left pole]
- **2-3**: very [left pole]
- **4**: somewhat [left pole]
- **5**: neutral / both slightly
- **6**: somewhat [right pole]
- **7-8**: very [right pole]
- **9-10**: extremely [right pole]

## Calibration discipline

**Default to mid-range scores.** Most writing isn't 10/10 on any dimension. Insisting on extreme scores produces miscalibrated profiles for typical drafts. A piece that scores 7/10 toward casual is more accurately profiled than one that scores 10/10 — most "casual" writing is *somewhat* casual, not maximally casual.

**Reserve 9-10 and 0-1 for genuinely extreme cases.** Use them when warranted, not as the default. The discipline guards against overweighting one or two surface markers — a casual contraction or an em-dash doesn't push casual to a 10. The discipline does NOT prohibit extreme scores when the writing is unambiguously extreme.

**Carve-out for genuinely extreme writing.** Writing that is unambiguously formal AND unambiguously serious AND unambiguously matter-of-fact CAN score in the 1-2 range on multiple dimensions — that's an accurate profile, not a calibration failure. Examples: post-incident engineering reports, legal disclaimers, formal academic abstracts. Multiple extreme scores are warranted when each axis is independently extreme. The "mid-range default" rule prevents fake extremes from a few surface markers. It does not require the score to lie about a piece that genuinely sits at an extreme on multiple axes.

The test: for each extreme score, can you cite 2-3 supporting passages that genuinely show extremity on *that* axis, not just markers that overlap with another axis? If yes, the extreme is calibrated. If you're scoring extreme on multiple axes from the same one or two markers, that's the failure mode.

**Score on the writing, not the writer's intent.** If the writer says they meant the piece to be funny but the prose lands serious, score it serious. The profile describes the writing as it reads, not as it was meant.

## One message, many possible tones

The same factual message reads as four very different tones depending on calibration. Source illustration (error message):

- **Serious + formal + respectful + matter-of-fact:** *"We apologize, but we are experiencing a problem."*
- **Casual** (only one dimension shifted): *"We're sorry, but we're experiencing a problem on our end."*
- **Casual + enthusiastic:** *"Oops! We're sorry, but we're experiencing a problem on our end."*
- **Casual + funny + irreverent:** *"What did you do!? You broke it! (Just kidding. We're experiencing a problem on our end.)"*

Each shift in dimension produces a recognizably different brand voice, even though the core message ("an error has occurred") is identical.

## How dimensions interact

Dimensions are roughly orthogonal — you can score a piece independently on each — but certain combinations produce recognizable archetypes:

- **Casual + Serious + Respectful + Matter-of-Fact** → trusted-friend-explaining-something-difficult
- **Formal + Serious + Respectful + Enthusiastic** → academic-lecturer-clearly-loves-the-subject
- **Casual + Funny + Irreverent + Enthusiastic** → late-night-show-monologue
- **Casual + Serious + Irreverent + Matter-of-Fact** → curmudgeonly-domain-expert

When the synthesis paragraph (optional after the four scored dimensions) helps the user understand the writing, name the archetype the dimensions produce together.

## Describers and anti-tones

**Describers** are refined adjectives that go beyond the dimension label. "Casual" is the dimension. "Conversational, lived-in, plainspoken" are describers.

**Anti-tones** name what the writing is *not* — usually adjacent risks the writer has avoided. "Casual without being sloppy / lazy / too-familiar" sharpens the casual rating by exclusion.

Up to 3 of each per dimension. Fewer if the writing is genuinely undifferentiated on that axis.
