# Landing Page Anatomy

How landing pages compose. Reference for `modes/landing-page`. Covers section-by-section structure, the conversion logic each section serves, and the calibration differences across landing-page types (sales, opt-in, product page).

A landing page is dense, scannable, and purposeful. Every section earns its place by doing a specific job in the conversion path. Sections that don't earn their place produce friction and dropoff. The anatomy below is the producer's view — what each section does, how the parts compose, what kills a landing page.

## The eight sections of a long-form landing page

Most long-form landing pages use a recognizable section sequence. Different audiences and offers warrant different emphasis, but the eight sections cover the bulk of conversion-shaping work.

**Section 1 — Hero / above-the-fold.** The first thing the reader sees. Includes a headline (the specific outcome the offer produces, or the pain it solves), a sub-headline (clarifies or de-risks the headline), and usually a single CTA. The hero buys the next 30 seconds of reader attention. Specificity wins here above all — vague hero claims produce immediate dropoff.

**Section 2 — Problem / pain.** The reader's current situation, named in their own language. The problem section earns reader trust by demonstrating that the writer understands the reader's experience. Pain-triplet structures are common ("Your X is suffering. Your Y is suffering. Your Z is suffering"). The problem section is also where the audience profile from the brief shows up — what specific pain, what specific stuck place.

**Section 3 — Promise / transformation.** What changes if the reader engages with the offer. Stated as the outcome, not the offer's features. *"Claims approved in days, not months"* not *"Our 9-step methodology."* The promise establishes the gap between the reader's current state (problem) and their desired state (promise). The offer is the bridge.

**Section 4 — How it works / mechanism.** The specific approach or system that produces the promise. This is where Todd Brown's "unique mechanism" work pays off — the more specific and credible the mechanism, the more believable the promise. For pages with a named mechanism (a branded system, a proprietary method, a specific framework the offer is built on), the section names the mechanism and explains how it works in 200-400 words.

**Section 5 — Social proof.** Testimonials, case studies, named customers, specific results. Social proof handles the implicit "but does this actually work?" objection. Specific named cases outperform anonymous quotes. Quoted dollar amounts and timelines outperform generic praise.

**Section 6 — Offer.** The specifics of what's being sold. What's included, the price, any guarantees, any bonuses, the access timeline. The offer section is where direct-response copy traditionally stacks value-additions ("Plus you get X. Plus you get Y. Plus a free second seat..."). The stack is calibrated to the offer's actual value, not padded for the sake of length.

**Section 7 — Objection handling / FAQ.** The common reasons the reader hasn't bought yet, addressed directly. "But I already have X" / "But I'm not technical enough" / "But my situation is different" — each objection named and resolved. Strong objection-handling sections often beat their own competition because most landing pages skip this section entirely.

**Section 8 — Close / final CTA.** The last impression and the action. The close restates the promise concisely, names the deadline or scarcity if applicable, and presents the CTA one last time. Often paired with a final social-proof quote or a personal sign-off from the writer.

## Section variations by page type

Not every landing page uses all eight sections. The page type informs which sections are essential and which can compress or skip.

**Long-form sales page (warm traffic).** All eight sections, often with section 5 (social proof) and section 7 (objection handling) developed at length. Length: 2,000-5,000 words. Audience knows the writer — the page is converting decided-but-not-yet-committed readers.

**Short sales page (cold traffic).** Sections 1, 2, 3, 6, 8 (hero, problem, promise, offer, close). Sections 4, 5, 7 compressed or omitted. Length: 600-1,500 words. Audience is meeting the writer for the first time — less context to load, more first-impression pressure.

**Opt-in page (lead magnet).** Sections 1, 3, 6, 8 (hero, promise, offer, close). Section 2 (problem) sometimes a single line. Sections 4, 5, 7 often compressed to a single line each or omitted. Length: 200-600 words. The "offer" is the lead magnet (free download, free workshop), and the friction the page handles is just "give us your email" — which is low friction, so the page doesn't need to do as much work.

**Product page (e-commerce).** Sections 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 (hero, promise, mechanism, social proof, offer). Sections 2, 7, 8 often compressed. Length: 300-1,200 words. The reader knows what category of thing they're shopping for — the page differentiates this product from alternatives.

**Webinar / event registration page.** Sections 1, 3, 6, 8 (hero, promise, offer = the event details, close). Section 2 sometimes. Section 4 (the mechanism = "what we'll cover") usually present. Length: 400-1,000 words. The friction is registering, which is medium-low. The page does enough to commit the reader without overdoing.

The brief specifies the page type. Writing honors it.

## What each section earns

Sections earn their place by handling a specific conversion concern. When a section doesn't handle its concern, the section is dead weight.

**Hero earns** the next 30 seconds of attention. If the hero is generic, the reader doesn't read the next section. Specificity in the hero is the highest-leverage move on the entire page.

**Problem earns** reader trust by demonstrating understanding. A problem section that names pain in vague terms ("you're frustrated with X") fails to land. A problem section that names specific pain in the reader's own language ("you're spending Sunday nights chasing supplements that should have been approved on first submission") earns trust.

**Promise earns** the reader's investment in reading further. The promise must feel both desirable and achievable. Desirable but unbelievable produces dropoff. Achievable but unexciting produces no investment. The promise threading is delicate.

**Mechanism earns** belief in the promise. Without a specific mechanism, the promise reads as marketing fluff. With a specific, named, credible mechanism, the promise becomes plausible.

**Social proof earns** trust beyond the writer's own claims. Specific named cases earn more than anonymous testimonials. Quoted dollar amounts earn more than generic praise.

**Offer earns** the reader's evaluation of the deal. The offer section is where the reader decides whether the price-to-value math works for them. Stacked value-additions help when each addition is genuinely valuable. Padding hurts when the additions feel like filler.

**Objection handling earns** the conversion of readers who almost-but-don't-quite. Most landing pages skip this section, which means strong objection-handling is competitive advantage.

**Close earns** the action. The reader who's read this far is ready to decide. The close needs to close. A weak close produces "I'll think about it" exits at the moment of decision.

## What kills a landing page

Common failure modes writing flags during production.

**Generic hero.** The headline could apply to any product in the category. *"The best way to manage your pipeline."* / *"Transform your business with our solution."* Replaces with specifics from the offer and audience profile.

**Buried promise.** The promise is hidden in section 4. The reader who scrolled past sections 2-3 didn't see what's actually being offered. The promise belongs near the top, often as the headline or sub-headline.

**Mechanism without belief.** The mechanism is named ("our proprietary X Method") but not explained. Naming without explaining produces "marketing fluff" reaction. The mechanism section needs to actually explain how the thing works.

**Social proof that says nothing specific.** Testimonials like "this changed my life" / "I love this product" / "highly recommend." No specific result, no specific case, no specific name. Strong social proof has numbers, names, and outcomes.

**Offer stack that feels like filler.** "Plus you get X! Plus Y! Plus Z!" where X, Y, Z are minor add-ons. Stacks work when each addition earns its place. Padded stacks signal "the actual offer isn't strong enough."

**Wrong CTA.** The CTA promises something the page didn't set up, or the CTA's destination differs from what the reader expected. *"Get instant access"* on a page that doesn't actually deliver instant access produces friction at the moment of click.

**Close that trails off.** The final section just stops. No CTA, no urgency, no last impression. Strong closes restate the promise, name the action, and land the last line.

## Length and pacing

Long-form landing pages need pacing as much as length. Sections that read as walls of text lose readers regardless of substance. Calibrations:

Paragraph length runs 1-3 sentences typically, with occasional longer paragraphs for sections (like mechanism explanation) where the substance warrants it. Single-sentence paragraphs work for emphasis or pacing.

Section lengths vary by job. Hero: 50-150 words. Problem: 100-300 words. Promise: 50-150 words. Mechanism: 200-400 words. Social proof: variable, often 300-600 if developed. Offer: 200-500 words. Objection handling: variable, often 300-700 if developed. Close: 100-200 words.

Subheadings break up sections so readers can scan. Bullet points work for genuine enumerations (what's included in the offer, the steps of the mechanism) but should not substitute for prose argument.

CTAs appear at multiple points across long-form pages. Above-the-fold, after the promise, after the offer, in the close. Readers who decide at any point shouldn't have to scroll to act.
